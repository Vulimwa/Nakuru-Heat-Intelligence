# Nakuru Urban Heat Observatory Knowledge Base

## Table of Contents

## 1. Overview

The Nakuru Urban Heat Observatory monitors spatial and temporal patterns of Surface Urban Heat Island Intensity (SIUHI), heat-class extent, population exposure, urban heat hotspots, land-cover relationships, and the spatial cooling association of Lake Nakuru.

### Study period
2021–2026

### Core interpretation principle

SIUHI describes relative surface thermal conditions derived from satellite-based surface temperature analysis. It is **not the same as air temperature** and should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of human thermal comfort.

In general:
- More positive SIUHI values indicate relatively warmer surface conditions.
- More negative SIUHI values indicate relatively cooler surface conditions.
- Comparisons between years are most useful when the same processing methodology, study boundary, seasonal period, classification approach, and data sources have been maintained.

The results below are the project's analyzed values and should be treated as the authoritative numerical knowledge for this observatory.

## Interpretation of hotspot results

The hotspot classification identifies different temporal behaviors of high-heat locations.

950 features are classified as Persistent Hotspot. These represent locations that repeatedly meet the project's hotspot criteria across the analyzed period.

### New Hotspot
460 features are classified as New Hotspot. These represent locations where hotspot conditions emerged according to the analysis criteria.

### No Longer Very High
934 features are classified as No Longer Very High. These represent locations that were previously associated with very high heat conditions but no longer meet that category under the analysis.

The Persistent Hotspot category has the largest count among the three categories.

### Important caution

Hotspot categories describe the classification produced by the project's spatial-temporal hotspot analysis. They should not be interpreted as permanent physical conditions or as proof of a particular cause. Local land cover, development, surface materials, vegetation, moisture, and other environmental factors may contribute to observed patterns.

# 12. Methodological Context and Limitations

The observatory is based on satellite-derived surface thermal analysis and spatial analysis over the 2021–2026 period. The project uses SIUHI as a measure of relative surface heat conditions and combines it with heat-class mapping, population exposure, hotspot analysis, land-cover comparison, and distance-band analysis around Lake Nakuru.

The results should be interpreted with awareness of the following limitations:

- SIUHI represents surface thermal conditions, not near-surface air temperature.
- Satellite observations describe conditions captured by the selected imagery and processing period; they do not represent every moment of the year.
- Population exposure is spatially modeled/estimated and does not automatically indicate individual exposure duration or health impact.
- Land-cover comparisons identify associations between surface categories and SIUHI but do not by themselves prove causation.
- Distance from Lake Nakuru is an explanatory spatial gradient, but other environmental and urban factors can vary with distance.
- Hotspot categories depend on the project's hotspot-analysis criteria and input data.
- Differences between years should be interpreted in the context of data acquisition, seasonal conditions, preprocessing, and classification consistency.

# 14. Interventions for Sustainable Urban Cooling in Nakuru

Based on the empirical spatial and thermal findings of the Nakuru Urban Heat Observatory (2021–2026), sustainable urban cooling interventions are categorized into target zones and actionable nature-based and engineering solutions:

1. Nature-Based Solutions (NBS) & Urban Greening
- Canopy Cover Expansion in Built-Up Hotspots: Given that Built-up SIUHI reached 3.05 in 2026 and persistent hotspots count 950 locations, targeted tree planting with native high-transpiration canopy trees (e.g., Acacia xanthophloea, Markhamia lutea) in dense residential and commercial wards reduces localized surface temperatures.
- Preservation & Expansion of Riparian Buffers: Lake Nakuru's 0-500 m distance band exhibits an ultra-cool SIUHI (-9.16 in 2026). Safeguarding shoreline wetlands, herbaceous buffers (-4.29 SIUHI), and river corridors (e.g., Njoro, Makalia, Enderit rivers) maintains the natural lake-breeze cooling buffer.
- Grassland and Soil Vegetation Restoration: Bare / Sparse Vegetation had the highest mean SIUHI in 2026 (3.30). Soil stabilization, cover cropping, and urban park grassland maintenance can mitigate extreme bare-ground thermal emissions.

2. Built-Environment & Cool Materials
- Cool Roofs and Reflective Surfacing: Converting dark corrugated metal and asphalt roofing in built-up areas to high-albedo coatings or light reflective materials significantly lowers surface heat absorption.
- Permeable Pavements & Shaded Corridors: Replacing impervious concrete walkways and parking yards with permeable paving blocks allows soil moisture retention and evaporative cooling.
- Passive Building Design & Urban Shade Canopies: Architectural guidelines encouraging cross-ventilation, external window overhangs, and shaded streetscapes reduce heat gain in high-exposure residential quarters.

3. Targeted Spatial Priority Wards
- High Exposure Priority Zones: Interventions should prioritize the wards housing the 375,646+ residents exposed to Very High heat conditions, particularly in expanding informal and high-density urban settlements.
- Persistent Hotspot Mitigation: The 950 persistent hotspot features identified in the 2021–2026 analysis require immediate urban forestry and cool pavement retrofits.

# 15. Recommendations & Guides for Urban Heat Management

This guide provides practical recommendations for urban planners, municipal decision-makers, public health officers, and environmental researchers in Nakuru:

1. Guide for Urban Planners & Municipal Authorities
- Integrate SIUHI Maps into Zoning Frameworks: Utilize the Observatory's heat-class spatial boundaries and land-cover thermal relationships when approving residential expansion, industrial zoning, and infrastructure projects.
- Mandate Minimum Green Plot Ratios: Enforce urban planning bylaws requiring commercial developments and housing estates to set aside 20–30% of site area for permeable vegetative cover or tree canopy.
- Protect Lake Cooling Corridors: Maintain open ventilation and green corridors connecting Lake Nakuru's cool perimeter (0–1000 m distance bands) to the inner city to facilitate nocturnal lake breeze cooling.

2. Guide for Public Health & Community Heat Resilience
- Target Heat Vulnerability Outreach: Focus heat advisories and community cooling stations in high-density wards with elevated population exposure (e.g., peak exposure years reached over 407,000 residents in Very High heat zones).
- Urban Agriculture and Community Gardens: Encourage community-managed urban gardens in vacant lots to replace bare/sparse ground with productive, transpiration-cooling vegetation.

3. Guide for Environmental Researchers & Monitoring
- Continuous Satellite & In-Situ Thermal Tracking: Combine continuous satellite SIUHI monitoring with ground-level microclimate sensor networks to track ambient air temperatures alongside surface thermal dynamics.
- Annual Hotspot Evaluation: Track emerging new hotspots (460 identified in 2021–2026) and monitor the efficacy of cooling retrofits over time.
